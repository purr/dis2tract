Why the scroll wins

If you keep ending up back in the feed, that is not a character flaw. The feed was built without a bottom, and your brain is doing what brains do. The good news: a few small, boring changes work better than willpower.

A feed with no bottom

Pages used to end. At the end you made a choice: next page, or something else. Infinite scroll removed that moment. The person who designed it, Aza Raskin, has said for years that he regrets it, and repeated that under oath in 2026 at a trial against Meta.1 Autoplay does the same for video: the next one starts before you decide anything.

Dopamine is wanting, not liking

Dopamine is often called the pleasure chemical. Research from Kent Berridge's lab points the other way: dopamine drives the pull toward the next thing (wanting), not the enjoyment of it (liking).2 That is why you can keep scrolling past posts you do not even enjoy. So the useful question is not how to lower your dopamine. It is how to change what pulls you and what stops you.

The urge is usually standing in for something

Boredom is the best-documented trigger: a meta-analysis found it linked with digital media use, most strongly with general internet use.3 Loneliness, tiredness, stress and a task you are avoiding show up in the same place. The scroll answers none of them, which is why an hour of it rarely feels like rest.

What research says helps

36%

of attempts to open a target app ended with people closing it again, once a short pause with a way out stood in between.4

57%

fewer app openings after six weeks, in a field study with 280 people.4

  • A pause with a way out. In that same study, the option to back out mattered most. The waiting time helped less, and a reminder message on its own did nothing.4
  • Breaks you choose yourself. People who were offline more than usual felt better, more energetic and more connected, but only when the break was their own decision. Breaks that felt imposed could even lower their mood.5
  • An if-then plan. "When I want to scroll, I will do ten pushups." Plans in this form cut phone use in a randomized trial with drivers.6
  • Rules you can live with. In Stanford's HabitLab study of 1,654 people, stronger interventions reduced use more, and also made more people uninstall.7 A blocker you keep beats a perfect one you delete.
